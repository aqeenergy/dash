## Objective

The TEMPUS (Tiegelfreies ElektroMagnetisches Prozessieren Unter Schwerelosgkeit) facility is a research instrument designed to perform electromagnetic levitation studies in reduced gravity. TEMPUS was a joint undertaking between DARA, the German space agency, and the Microgravity Science and Applications Division of NASA. Specific objectives for the different investigations carried out by US Principal Investigators using this facility is categorized by different classes:
Class A: Studies of undercooling phenomena and the kinetics of nucleation (Prof. M. Flemings, MIT, Prof. R. Bayuzick, Vanderbilt Univ), Class B: Measurement of specific heat in liquid metals using an AC calorimetry technique (Prof W. Johnson, CALTECH), Class C: Measurement of viscosity and surface tension of undercooled metals (Prof. J. Szkeley, Prof. M. Flemings, MIT), Class D: Measurement of thermal expansion in undercooled materials (Prof W. Johnson, CALTECH).
In addition to the US Principal Investigators, available data from European investigations performed on TEMPUS and captured here include Viscosity and Surface Tension of Undercooled Metallic Melts (Dr. Egry, DLR, Germany), Thermophysical Properties of Undercooled Metallic Melts (Dr. Egry, DLR, Germany), Thermal Expansion of Glass forming Metallic Alloys in the Undercooled State (Dr. Samwer, Georg-August-Universitat Gottingen, Germany), and Thermodynamics and Glass Formation Undercooled Liquid Alloys (Dr. Wunderlich and Dr. Fecht, University of Ulm, Germany).

## Hypothesis

Levitation, positioning and processing of materials which are not in direct contact with container surfaces, provides a unique opportunity to study a number of phenomena. In particular, the lack of contact with container walls reduces the possibility for heterogeneous nucleation in studies of the processing of molten materials. The study of the properties and solidification of undercooled materials is facilitated by containerless processing.

## Approach

Metallic samples of 7 or 8 mm diameter are positioned in the TEMPUS coils by electromagnetic forces and melted by induced currents. A ''squeezing'' force is then applied to a stably positioned liquid sample to induce oscillations. The rate of decay of the amplitude of these oscillations can be observed in order to measure the viscosity at different temperatures, while the frequency of the oscillation modes is used to deduce the surface tension at these temperatures. Measurements are planned on reactive, refractory melts (Zr), high-viscosity eutectics (ZrNi, PdSi), and industrial alloys (Fe-Cr-Ni) in addition to standard materials for comparison (Au, Ni). In addition, mathematical modeling designed to give a detailed understanding of what can be expected from prevailing conditions in the flight experiments was also developed.

## Research impacts

The accurate determination of the thermophysical properties of liquid materials is important for fundamental and practical reasons, including verification of atomistic models of the liquid state, nucleation studies of phase transition, and modeling of materials processing operations. In addition, these studies often focus on advanced materials such as metallic glass forming materials. Metallic glasses are an industrially important new class of materials. They are ductile, isotropic and corrosion resistant and are used in magnetic storage media films.
