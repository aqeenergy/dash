## Objective

The ACE-T7 experiment involves the design and assembly of complex 3D structures from small particles suspended within a fluid medium. These so-called “self-assembled colloidal structures”, are vital to the design of advanced optical materials and active devices. The objective of  ACE-T7 was to utilize confocal microscopy for time- and space-resolved, 3D imaging of non-spherical colloids whose phases can be controlled by adding depletants and/or adjusting spatial temperature changes around a particular location. The research goal is to produce cubic colloidal structures, and study the dynamics of crystal nucleation and growth. ACE-T7 varied the size of the depletant in different samples with the goal of seeing the effect on crystallization.  The imaging goal was to observe crystallites and resolve particle centroid positions with less than 20% error.

## Hypothesis

It is hypothesized that under certain conditions, the particles should self-organize into crystals with simple cubic symmetry, which is set by the size of the nano-size depletant. Increasing particle number density using a temperature gradient, i.e., thermophoresis, is expected to induce formation of crystallites. Three-dimensional structures that are impossible to create or reform on earth due to sedimentation issues (high density contrast between particles and fluid) should be observed in microgravity.

## Approach

The ACE-T7 experiment used the Fluids Integrated Rack (FIR) and the Light Microscopy Module (LMM) with enhancements. ACE-T7 utilized two sample cartridges, each having three temperature-controlled  capillaries for a total of six samples; each capillary volume has 50 mm length, 2.0 mm ID width, and 0.2 mm ID height, with automated mixing capabilities using magnetic stir bars embedded in the capillaries. Each sample volume was about 20 μl. Each cartridge could be heated or cooled uniformly via Peltier temperature control. The concentrations of hollow silica microcubes and silica nanoparticle depletants are varied to study the effect that each of these parameters have on the dynamics of crystal nucleation and growth in microgravity. In total, six capillaries are prepared, numbered C1-C6. In capillaries C1-C4, the depletant concentration is kept constant at a concentration that produces cubic crystals in a timely manner on Earth (within 1 hour), while the concentration of hollow silica cubes is varied. In capillaries C5 and C6, the depletant concentration is increased and two different concentration of hollow silica cubes are used. After homogenization of each sample using the magnetic stirrer, the particles are left undisturbed to crystallize. Samples were preliminarily observed with a 2.5X objective, followed by measurements with 10X, 63X (air), 63X (oil), and 100X (oil) objectives.

## Research impacts

Future space exploration may use self-assembly and self-replication to make materials and devices that can repair themselves. Self-assembly and evolutionarily optimized functional units are key to long-duration space voyages which will require  use self-assembly and self-replication to make materials and devices that can repair themselves.  This research will enable design and development of functional colloidal structures for new devices for chemical energy, communication, and photonics, including photonic materials to control and manipulate light. Such materials might include photonic crystals with programmed distributions of defects. Optical technology utilizing such materials may offer intriguing solutions to unavoidable heat generation and bandwidth limitations facing the computer industry. The beginning of this process is understanding the basic interactions between micro- and nanoscale particles, and how to control them using external sources such as temperature and light.
