# PSI-80 · LMMBIO-3 — Light Microscopy Module Biophysics-3

- **Research area:** Biophysics
- **Sub-area:** Proteins
- **Hardware:** Light Microscopy Module (LMM)
- **Flight platform:** International Space Station (ISS)
- **Project type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Keywords:** Crystallization · Crystal Growth Rate · macromolecular crystals · protein structure · Protein Crystallization · advanced photon source · LMMBio · biophysical analysis

## Objective

The primary objectives of this investigation are:
1. Use molecular biology to produce closely related protein constructs that display differences in growth rate dispersion during crystallization. Measure the intermolecular ‘attractiveness’ of each construct, then model the inter-particle forces, diffusion and convection.
2. Measure and compare the growth rate dispersion of ground and microgravity grown crystals. Growth rate dispersion will be measured for ground control crystals using routine crystallization apparatus. In addition, growth rate dispersion will be measure for ground and
microgravity grown crystals using the flight apparatus.
3. Characterize the quality of both ground and microgravity grown crystals. Sophisticated X-ray analysis along with X-ray structural data collection will be used to quantify and characterize crystal quality.
 4. Link the knowledge about inter-particle forces, measured acceleration levels, calculated flow rates and the resultant X-ray analysis to develop a model and predictive ground-based tests to identify those samples producing crystals on the ground that would be improved in microgravity.

## Hypothesis

Removing or greatly reducing the effects of buoyancy-driven convection and sedimentation in microgravity will result in slower, diffusion-controlled molecular transport that can yield larger crystals with fewer defects.
It is postulated that growth rate dispersion is reduced in microgravity due to the diffusive rather than convective flow regime that delivers macromolecules to the crystal. Based on previous experimental observations it is hypothesized that the presence of growth rate dispersion in macromolecular crystals grown on the ground is an indicator of crystals that can be improved when grown in microgravity.

## Approach

Approach: Several cassettes were configured with cassettes identically configured containing three samples, one set in capillaries 1-6, another sample in capillaries 7-12, and the third in capillaries 13-18. Six constructs and the T4 wild type (WT) were loaded into the capillaries at various concentrations and frozen to -80C. The samples were thawed and observed in the LMM facility on board the ISS.

Several cassettes were identically configured containing three samples, one set in capillaries 1-6, another sample in capillaries 7-12, and the third in capillaries 13-18. The capillaries were loaded so that capillaries 1-6 contained theWT* construct, capillaries 7-12 the S44E WT* construct and finally, capillaries 13-18 the S44F WT* construct. The cassettes were kept at -80°C until moving to the space station microscope facility. The capillaries were filled as follows:
• Capillaries 1-6 with 50 mg/ml WT* in 0.1 M Zn(C 2 H3 O2 )2 , 0.1 M Tris, pH 8, 35% PEG 8K, and 0.1 M NaCl
• Capillaries 7-12 with 64 mg/ml S44E WT* in 0.05 M NaC 2 H3 O2 0.1 M Tris, pH 8 30% PEG 8K and 15% glycerol
• Capillaries 13-18 with 53 mg/ml S44F WT* in 0.2 M Zn(C 2 H3 O2 )2 , 0.1 M NaC2H3O2, pH 5 and 20% PEG 20K.
On return to earth X-ray analysis of samples took place using the IMCA (Industrial Macromolecular Crystallography Association) beamline of the Advanced Photon Source.

## Research impacts

Research Impacts/Earth Benefits: X-ray crystallography is the main method scientists use to study the molecular structures of biology, but it is difficult to crystallize proteins, with only a poor success rate even in the best laboratories. This investigation uses ground and microgravity-based observations to predict which proteins would benefit from crystallization in microgravity, improving the information that may be obtained from them.

Long-duration protein crystal growth experiments on the ISS with photo documentation, and subsequent analysis of the comparisons between 1g and μg crystals, will enable a more complete understanding of why proteins and other macromolecules often form more perfect crystals in microgravity than they do on earth. If it could be predicted which macromolecular samples could be improved by crystal growth in microgravity, then the true potential of this medium could be exploited in an efficient manner. Structural biology of protein-protein complexes and integral membrane proteins are currently a high NIH priority due to their importance for systems biology, disease mechanisms and structure-guided drug development. Hauptman Woodward Medical Research Institute has a data archive of millions of time-resolved crystallization images (from over 14,000 different biological macromolecules) that could be used to identify candidates likely to benefit from crystallization in microgravity.
