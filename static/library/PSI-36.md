## Objective

Zero-G Studies of Few-Body and Many-Body Physics (Cold Atom Lab - Few-Body Physics) investigates the dynamics of interacting ultracold atoms under varying conditions. The dynamics of few-body systems comprised of just three or four atoms can strongly influence the collective behavior of quantum gases and play an important role in development of quantum gas applications. Modern laser cooling techniques are used to chill atoms to near absolute zero temperatures. At these temperatures, atoms behave like matter waves, according to the laws of quantum mechanics. Fully automated data runs, each lasting about one minute, acquire images of ultracold atom clouds under varying conditions. The acquired data fills a critical need for the development of detailed and precise atomic physics models. The development of these models is performed in theoretical studies accompanying the data gathering campaigns with the CAL instrument. Studying the behavior and interaction of few atoms in microgravity could provide important data for developing a precise theory describing quantum gases on a microscopic level. The knowledge gained in this study also aids in the development of future quantum technologies for which a detailed microscopic understanding of few-body physics is essential. Examples of future quantum technologies include, among others, atom interferometers for inertial navigation or precision measurements of gravity and sensitive Terahertz (THz) detectors for astronomy.

## Hypothesis

Ultracold atoms in microgravity provide a unique environment for the study of complex few-body phenomena. One of the surprising predictions for quantum mechanical few-body systems is the existence of universality, i.e. the idea that highly intricate phenomena can be governed by underlying principles that are agnostic to specific microscopic realizations. As a concrete example, Efimov physics predicts the emergence of an infinite series of exotic three-body bound states when the interactions between the atoms are made stronger and stronger. It is predicted that these Efimov states are connected by universal scaling relations governing their energy and their size. Our experiments aim to provide essential benchmark data to test this universality hypothesis. By measuring two members of this infinite series, scaling relations can be probed and can be compared to some of the most advanced few-body theories to date. This will be a steppingstone towards the investigation of a plethora of associated few-body phenomena, with the ultimate goal of significantly advancing the boundary of our understanding and paving the way for the development of new quantum technologies.

## Approach

The Zero-G Studies of Few-Body and Many-Body Physics investigation uses rubidium (87Rb) and potassium (39K and 41K) samples in the Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL) aboard the International Space Station (ISS).
The approach for the investigation comprises two main aspects: the creation of ultracold atomic gases and the subsequent manipulation and probing of these gases to study few-body physics and exotic quantum states. Modern laser-cooling techniques are used to cool atoms to near absolute zero temperatures. At these temperatures, atoms behave like matter waves, according to the laws of quantum mechanics. Fully automated data runs, each lasting about one minute, acquire images of ultracold atom clouds under varying conditions.

1. Creation of Ultracold Atomic Gas:
    • Laser Cooling: Atoms are released into a vacuum chamber and collected into a magneto-optical trap (MOT), where they are initially cooled using laser cooling techniques. This technique is based on the rapid scattering of light off atoms and can cool a cloud of atoms to temperatures below 1x10-3 K. An additional magnetic field applied in conjunction with the lasers concentrates the cold atoms in a specific region of the vacuum apparatus, called the source chamber. The MOT is the starting point for further cooling and experimental preparation.
    • State Preparation: Using further laser manipulations, the atoms are then prepared in a state that seeks to occupy regions of low magnetic fields. By applying a suitable magnetic field geometry with a moving magnetic minimum, the cloud of atoms is transported to a so-called “science region.” Magnetic fields generated by a chip with current-carrying traces provide a trap for the atoms in the science region.
    • Evaporative Cooling: With the atoms trapped in the science region, evaporative cooling is performed by selectively removing the hottest rubidium atoms from the cloud. The remaining atoms then rethermalize at a lower temperature. The selective removal of the hottest rubidium atoms is achieved by applying suitably tuned radiofrequency radiation. Potassium atoms are cooled by being in thermal contact with the actively cooled rubidium atoms, in a process called sympathetic cooling, where rubidium atoms act as a cold bath of atoms. With sufficient evaporative cooling, the clouds of rubidium and potassium atoms can undergo a phase transition to a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC), providing a macroscopic, quantum mechanical object. However, pronounced quantum mechanical effects can already be observed before reaching this phase transition.
    • Further sample preparation: After the preparation of the ultracold sample, further preparation steps are employed for the proposed studies. These steps include changes to the trapping strength, changes of the internal states of the atoms, and the application of suitable homogeneous magnetic fields.

2. Manipulation and Detection:
    • Manipulation Techniques: The ultracold gas is manipulated using laser, radiofrequency, and/or microwave pulses. In addition, homogeneous magnetic fields are used to shift internal energy levels of the atoms.  These techniques are designed to adjust the pairwise interactions as needed for the preparation of exotic quantum states.
    • Time-of-Flight Imaging: After a predetermined time-of-flight during which the cloud is released from the trap and can expand freely, the sample is imaged using absorption imaging techniques. For this, a short laser pulse is flashed onto the atoms, and the shadow cast by the atoms is imaged on a CCD camera using a custom microscope setup. This imaging process typically destroys the “quantumness” of the sample and concludes an experimental cycle. The apparatus then starts over with the preparation of a new sample. Many cycles are performed during a typical data-taking campaign, with a time scale of approximately one minute per run.

## Research impacts

Physics is the discipline that provides understanding of biology and chemistry at the most microscopic level, and the area within physics most relevant to chemistry and biology is “few-body physics.” It is an often neglected portion of physics, because it is so difficult to do. An important way to make progress is to simplify, simplify, simplify: to come up with model systems in which we can make progress that can later be applied to human-centric disciplines like biology, and develop exotic and useful new materials. A promising way to simplify is to study matter at lower temperature, and lower densities. The Cold-Atom Lab (CAL) flying in the International Space Station (ISS) is where we will reach the lowest possible temperatures, and low densities, to perform studies of simple, yet intricate (think “snowflakes”) clusters of three or four atoms.

Ultracold atomic gases are a key platform for a broad range of future quantum technologies, including ultraprecise sensing and timekeeping, novel medical imaging methods, secure communication schemes, high-performance computing, and development of exotic materials. A precise knowledge of few-body physics is necessary to fully realize the potential of quantum technologies. This project supports development of a detailed understanding of quantum mechanical few-body physics at the microscopic level, which may open new avenues for quantum gas applications in future space exploration. Such applications include inertial navigation and precision measurements using atom interferometry, or calibration standards for detectors used in astronomy.
