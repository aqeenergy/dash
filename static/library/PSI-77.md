# PSI-77 · ISSI — In Space Soldering Investigation

- **Research area:** Materials Science
- **Sub-area:** Soldering
- **Hardware:** Microgravity Workbench Area
- **Flight platform:** ISS
- **Project type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Keywords:** In-Space Soldering · Microgravity · surface tension gradient · Welding · porosity · kinematic and dynamic viscosity

## Objective

The primary objective is to better understand the quality of solders in a microgravity environment.  In microgravity, gas bubbles, or void spaces are found in the solder joint and at contact surfaces.  These voids reduce the thermal and electrical conductivity and also provide sites for crack initiation.  Bubbles have less chance to escape in the reduced gravity environment of space. Hence, by designing a systematic series of simple experiments, the objective was to investigate and better understand porosity development, surface wetting, and equilibrium shape formation while soldering in a reduced gravity environment.

## Hypothesis

Due to minimized buoyancy in the microgravity environment, it is hypothesized that one would expect additional porosity and enhanced surface tension contributions to porosity shape and mobility. Brazing, soldering, and welding operations in microgravity can be influenced greatly by both wetting and surface tension-driven flows, primary factors in achieving or not achieving a sound joint. For example, processes that rely on gravity-driven separations in the terrestrial environment, such as the removal of gas bubbles from a liquid, may rely on completely different interfacial phenomenon in microgravity.

## Approach

The proposed experiments basically consisted of taking pieces of silver-coated copper wire and bending/twisting them into simple configurations for test coupons. Selected lengths of Kester “44”® 60-40 rosin core solder (5 and 10 cm) were then cut and wrapped around a selected portion of the coupon, to be later melted by heat conduction through the wire using the kit’s soldering iron. A second set of experiments involved feeding 5 and 10 cm lengths of solder onto similar shaped coupons that were being heated by the iron. In principle, the soldering iron was never meant to come in contact with the molten solder. All in all, five soldering sessions were conducted on the ISS that culminated in 86 samples comprising seven different wire configurations, two different solder lengths (5 and 10 cm), and two different melt procedures (conduction melting of wrapped solder and conduction melting of solder being fed onto the wire). The melting experiments took place in the flame retardant Maintenance Work Area, a portable facility in the ISS used to make repairs and conduct other operations in the ISS. A digital camera is placed behind the samples to record the melting and subsequent solidification experiments. Video footage and still pictures were down linked after each experimental session.

## Research impacts

An investigation of soldering practices and behavior in a microgravity environment is relevant to our technological needs, particularly with regard to in-space fabrication and repair. The ISSI payload provides unique insight into microgravity soldering methods, which could play a fundamental role in maintaining the International Space Station as well as provide understanding of repair capabilities on future missions to the Moon and to Mars. The study of soldering in space could lead to better soldering techniques here on Earth.
