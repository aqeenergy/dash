## Objective

Development of Atom Interferometry Experiments for the International Space Station's Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL- Atom Interferometry) demonstrates using atom interferometry as a precision measurement technique on the space station. Atom interferometry is an ultra-sensitive measurement technique that splits the quantum wave function of an atom into two distinct wave packets that travel separate trajectories and then recombine. This technique, already demonstrated on the ground, should work better in space and could be useful for future space navigation systems.

## Hypothesis

This investigation hypothesizes that, combined with the current atomic cooling techniques, the implementation of expansion cooling will produce atoms with temperatures on the order of a trillionth of a degree above absolute zero (10^-12 K). With the unique settings provided by Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL) the maximum expansion and low temperature limits are able to be probed. Atomic interferometry can be used to measure the acceleration of cold atoms relative to the International Space Station, allowing investigators to explore using ultracold atoms as part of an inertial navigation system.

## Approach

This investigation uses rubidium (87Rb) and potassium (39K and 41K) samples in the Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL) aboard the International Space Station (ISS). The approach for the flight-based investigation comprises two main aspects: the creation of ultracold atomic gases and the subsequent manipulation and probing of these gases to study systematic mitigations. For dual-species operations the process is slightly more complicated, especially with the addition of a sympathetic cooling step, however, the general process is fairly standardized.
1.	Creation of Ultracold Atomic Gas:
•	Laser Cooling: Atoms are released into a vacuum chamber and collected into a magneto-optical trap (MOT), where they are initially cooled using laser cooling techniques. This technique cools the sample by essentially ‘pushing’ the laser against the motion of the atoms effectively lowering the momentum, and therefore the velocity, kinetic energy, and temperature, of the sample. This is followed by a brief stage of “optical molasses” during which the quadrupole magnetic field is turned off and the laser frequency is further detuned.
•	State Preparation: The sample is optically pumped to a low magnetic field-seeking quantum state. The state is then subjected to an intermediate quadrupole magnetic field which allows for the transferred from the MOT, located at the bottom of the science region, to an atom-chip-based trap, located at the top of the science region.
•	Evaporative Cooling: The atoms undergo evaporative cooling to below the critical temperature, leading to the formation of a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC). Evaporative cooling cools the sample by removing the highest-energy atom from the trap so that the remaining atoms re-thermalize at progressively lower temperatures.
•	Decompression and Release: The atom trap is slightly relaxed to further cool the atoms, eventually releasing them completely to allow free expansion. At this point, investigators can search for the primary signatures of a BECs for confirmation that one was formed. Formation of a BEC is typically associated with the behavior of the velocity distribution, such as the presence of a narrow peak centered at near zero velocity and the low velocity peak growing (greater fraction of atoms in this domain) as the temperature decreases.
2.	Manipulation and Probing:
•	Probing Techniques: The ultracold gas is probed using laser, radiofrequency, and/or microwave pulses. These techniques are designed to investigate the pairwise interactions and molecular dynamics crucial for mitigating systematics in high-precision measurements.
•	Time-of-Flight Imaging: After a predetermined time of flight, the sample is imaged using absorption imaging techniques. This process involves taking two images: one of the sample and a reference image post-destruction of the sample. As implied, this is the end of the experimental cycle for this specific BEC as the measurement process destroys the sample.

This approach is particularly suited for studying mitigation schemes for leading-order systematics that can affect future high-precision measurements with atom interferometers (AIs) in microgravity. This investigation utilizes the atom interferometry equipment aboard the CAL instrument for precision measurement experiments. The BEC formed according to the steps laid out in the Creation of Ultracold Atomic Gases section is split using an off resonance standing wave laser. The split waves packets will travel different trajectories, and then recombine after a secondary laser beam is applied. After the recombination, the atomic interference pattern can be collected. The flight-based results can be compared to theoretical investigations and ground studies done at JPL, enabling comprehensive understanding and development of systematic mitigation techniques.

## Research impacts

The development of precision inertial sensing techniques is useful for Earth-based as well as space-based navigation. Besides using direct sensing for inertial navigation, rotation sensing can also be useful for north-finding while gravity sensing can be used to tabulate local gravity variations and form a type of three-dimensional map for navigating.

These techniques also have many applications in geophysics. Gravity sensing can be used for oil and mineral exploration, while rotation sensing can detect dynamics in the Earth's core. Gravity sensing also has defense applications such as locating underground tunnels and potential screening cargo for high-density contraband or weapons.

Other precision measurement applications have less direct impact, but advance scientific knowledge. For instance, precision tune-out spectroscopy measurements of atomic matrix elements can be used to improve the interpretation of atomic parity violation experiments. These in turn impact our understanding of the standard model of particle physics and thus the nature of our universe. Direct benefits of such understanding can be hard to trace, but in general the continued advance of technological applications builds on advances in our fundamental knowledge.
