# PSI-187 · PWM — Plant Water Management

- **Research area:** Fluid Physics
- **Sub-area:** Fluid Dynamics
- **Hardware:** Plant Water Management (PWM) hardware
- **Flight platform:** International Space Station (ISS)
- **Project type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Keywords:** Root aeration · plant growth · Capillary-driven irrigation · Gas-liquid separation · Passive irrigation systems · Hydroponics · Life-support systems · Controlled-environment agriculture

## Objective

The primary objectives of the Plant Water Management (PWM) experiments were to:

a) Experimentally characterize and validate the use of passive capillary and  geometric control for plant watering in microgravity.
The research quantified the stability limits of capillary-driven flow under varying conditions, evaluated aeration and bubble management performance within root-zone analogs, and assessed start-up, shutdown, and drainage behaviors of the fluidic system on orbit and also correlated observed flow regimes and saturation levels with computational capillary flow models to improve predictive design tools for reduced-gravity systems.
b) Advance the PWM technology toward higher Technology Readiness Levels (TRL), demonstrating reliable, scalable configurations for integration into future space-based plant growth habitats.
The resulting data and models informed the design of next-generation, low-mass, low-power life-support and food-production systems for long-duration missions on the Moon, Mars, and beyond.

## Hypothesis

This research was based on the hypothesis that, in microgravity where gravity-driven fluid behavior is absent, capillary forces, surface tension, and geometric design could provide reliable water and nutrient delivery to plant root zones. Passive physical mechanisms replaced gravity in distributing and reclaiming fluids within the growth systems. Surface-wetting characteristics and channel geometry were designed to maintain root-zone hydration, facilitate aeration, and manage liquid–gas separation without pumps or active components. These mechanisms sustained water availability and oxygen exchange, preventing oversaturation or desiccation. The approach demonstrated that passive capillary systems could support consistent, low-maintenance plant watering and aeration in reduced-gravity environments.

## Approach

PWM employed passive capillary-based fluidic systems to deliver water and nutrients to plant root zones under microgravity aboard the ISS. Growth modules were designed with controlled channel geometries and surface-wetting properties to direct liquid flow, manage gas-liquid separation, and maintain root-zone aeration. Both hydroponic and soil-based configurations were tested to evaluate performance across different growth media. Measurements included monitoring liquid distribution, root-zone moisture levels, aeration efficiency, and bubble formation, alongside comparing observations to predictive capillary flow models. By systematically varying system geometry, wetting conditions, and operational parameters, the experiments defined design guidelines for robust, low-maintenance plant growth systems for space and analogous terrestrial applications.

## Research impacts

Demonstrating passive fluid management in plant growth systems is enabling reliable water and nutrient delivery in microgravity, supporting long-duration space missions.
Advancing capillary-based irrigation is reducing system mass, complexity, and power requirements. In relation to agriculture on Earth, results from this research are improving precision irrigation, conserving water, and enhancing root-zone aeration in hydroponic and soil systems. Generating validated models of capillary-driven flow is guiding efficient, resilient plant growth technologies. Supporting scalable, low-maintenance systems is fostering innovations in both space life-support and sustainable food production on Earth.
