# PSI-2 · MSRR GEDS — Multi-Scale Modeling and Experimentation on Liquid Phase Sintering in Gravity and Microgravity Environments

- **Research area:** Materials Science
- **Sub-area:** Liquid Phase Sintering
- **Hardware:** Low Gradient Furnace (LGF)
- **Flight platform:** International Space Station (ISS)
- **Project type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Keywords:** Metals · Sintering · Densification · Tungsten Heavy Alloy · GEDS · Grain Growth · Alloys · Distortion · Microstructure · MSRR · Porosity · Additive Manufacturing

## Objective

In this study, the compositions start with W-Ni-Cu-Mn powder mixtures. The solid-liquid ratio is adjusted via changes in the tungsten content from 70 to 90 wt %. Most of the tungsten remains solid during sintering. The liquid is formed from melting the mechanically alloyed Ni-Cu-Mn additive. The liquid dissolves up to 28 wt % tungsten at the sintering temperature, giving the desired semisolid system. In these experiments, changes are also made to the Ni:Cu ratio from 3:2 to 2:3 to slightly adjust the tungsten solubility. Seven cartridges with seven samples each are processed in microgravity. One cartridge is heated to the temperature where first significant densification occurs on Earth, 1180°C, and five of the cartridges are processed for progressively longer times at 1210°C. A seventh cartridge is for backup in case of an aborted run, otherwise it provides a replicate at 34 min at 1210°C. An early part of this research focused on developing a W-Ni-Cu-Mn alloy to allow lower sintering temperatures to accommodate downgraded furnace peak temperatures. The new alloys are tested using ground proof runs to gather ground data. Those results are contrasted with the microgravity data. Extensive testing is documented to show the experimental platform; tests involved thermal overload, vibration, equipment proofing, evaporation, transient runaway, and temperature verification trials. An early failure in the quartz ampoule led to redesign. Subsequent ground runs were conducted without failure. This report emphasizes the development of the experiments and details the densification and distortion data, and provides microstructure data. Modeling ideas were established early and awaited samples for verification. Much effort was directed to developing a time sequence whereby modeling data would be available for rheological variables associated with the solid-liquid-pore structures existing during sintering, namely extraction of the viscosity and strength variations during the 60 min of isothermal hold. The model is implemented in finite element analysis where stress on the sintering body is assessed using variations in the composition, component size, and gravity.

## Hypothesis

The idea of removing distortion during liquid phase sintering has much appeal. Loss of dimensional precision during sintering is a common industrial problem. Forming technologies allow for precise green bodies (green is the term adopted to indicate the component after forming, but prior to sintering). The body force due to gravity acts in the vertical direction and increases with height and density. The distortion problem is most evident in structures with protrusions and unsupported or cantilever features. Accordingly, for some liquid phase sintered components a majority of the production expense is in correcting dimensional issues after sintering, by use of coining, straightening, grinding, or machining. Many powder metallurgy components start with a green density of just 60 % of theoretical. Shaping is possible using a variety of options - die pressing, injection molding, warm extrusion, cold isostatic pressing, or slurry casting. High performance demands a high sintered density, often from 98 to 100 %. The change from 60 to 98 % density comes with about 15 % shrinkage in each dimension. However, as the component height increases gravity induces nonuniform shrinkage. The friction force at the component bottom increases. This friction force resists densification on the bottom surface, resulting in nonuniform dimensions, effectively distorting the green body. The economic attraction for sintering technologies is the delivery of net-shapes, avoiding post-sintering machining if possible. Unfortunately, it is easy to lose dimensional control, necessitating secondary operations that add greatly to the fabrication expense. In the ideal, tooling design would anticipate sintering shrinkage, even nonuniform shrinkage, enabling sintering to directly deliver final size. This would be possible with computer tools to design the tooling in anticipation of shrinkage and distortion. Our initial conjecture suggested that liquid phase sintering in microgravity would remove distortion, since shrinkage and microstructure should be uniform.

## Approach

The idea of removing distortion during liquid phase sintering has much appeal. Loss of dimensional precision during sintering is a common industrial problem. Forming technologies allow for precise green bodies (green is the term adopted to indicate the component after forming, but prior to sintering).  The body force due to gravity acts in the vertical direction and increases with height and density. In these trials, components of 12 mm in height are processed and analyzed for features such as density, dimensional variation (distortion), microstructure, grain size distribution, and pore agglomeration after return to Earth. Gravity normally produces sufficient substrate friction to locally resist shrinkage during sintering, giving an elephant foot geometry. Microgravity trails examine if densification is possible without distortion, the benefit being net-shape processing. For this research on gravity effects on distortion and densification during liquid phase sintering, the two primary metrics are nonuniform dimensional change and the elimination of pores. Along these lines, isotropic right circular cylinders are fabricated for sintering. Both the initial and sintered dimensions are measured over several locations to quantify densification (and shrinkage) and distortion. Densification is measured using dimensional shrinkage (up to 15 %), relying on the change in porosity divided by the initial porosity (37 %). The samples are initially 63 % dense. Distortion is calculated using multiple dimensional measurements over closely spaced locations and is expressed as the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by the mean size). For microstructure assessment to help explain the behavior, the samples are sectioned, mounted, and polished. Optical microscopy, quantitative microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and energy dispersive analysis of X-rays provide information on the microstructure, especially the pore and grain structure. Quantitative microstructure data include grain size, pore size, connectivity, and distributions for these features. The results are compared to models to validate constitutive laws. In prior research microgravity resulted in large pores, extensive distortion, and a low grain connectivity microstructure with smaller grains.

## Research impacts

Sintering is a heat treatment applied to powders with the intent of bonding the particles into a solid structure. It is widely applied to forming complex shapes for automotive, computer, metal cutting, oil and gas drilling, hand tools, firearms, and hardware. Tools is designed to shape the powder into a shape that is oversized to allow for shrinkage. With sufficiently high temperature, heat induces atomic motion and repositioning of solid to form bonds between the particles that strengthen the body. Usually that occurs via diffusion along interfaces such as grain boundary liquid films. In industrial production, the powder is shaped prior to sintering, such as by die compaction or injection molding, with subsequent sintering to produce a complex shape. Accordingly, sintering is frequently applied to high performance materials and is applied in practice over a wide range of products and industries from bricks to rocket engines. It is especially useful for high temperature materials, such as the refractory metals, including tungsten. If densification can be acheived without distortion, considerable expense is avoided by net-shape fabrication. In this sense, tremendous industrial benefit is possible using liquid phase sintering with densifcation but no distortion. In the US sintering technologies are responsible for about 60 kg of each automobile, and dozens of parts in each computer and cell phone. Liquid phase sintering is a desirable option, where in an alloy some liquid forms during the heating cycle (between the liquidus and solidus temperatures for the alloy). The idea of liquid phase sintering gained significance attention during the 1920s as a means to consolidate hard powders, such as cemented carbides (WC-Co), into high performance engineering structures, such as metal cutting tools, wire drawing dies, and oil-less bearings. The tungsten heavy alloys emerged at the same time. It is the latter system selected to demonstrate processing gains or losses associated with gravity.
