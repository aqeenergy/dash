## Objective

Microgravity Dynamics of Bubble-geometry Bose-Einstein Condensates (Cold Atom Lab - BEC Bubble Dynamics) aims to study Bose-Einstein Condensates (BECs) and degenerate Fermi gases (DFGs) confined to a the surface of an experimentally-controlled topologically-connected ''bubble.'' The limitations of gravitational sag have prevented terrestrial exploration of a quantum gas confined to such as surface. This investigation aims to test fundamental ideas in quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, and strongly-correlated many-body physics in condensed matter. Studying the behavior of quantum gas bubbles could provide insights for next-generation quantum sensors and simulators.

## Hypothesis

This investigation hypothesizes that radiofrequency dressing can be used to create a trap formed by a spherical or ellipsoidal shell structure capable of confining quantum gases to a “bubble” topology. Due to gravitational sag and trap distortion present in terrestrial experiments this trap topology has not been explored, and further, it is only able to be explored in the microgravity environment unique to the Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL). The low-energy excitations of such systems, as well as various theories on the physics of phase transitions will be investigated using this bubble-geometry.

## Approach

This investigation uses rubidium (87 Rb) and potassium (39K and 41K) samples in the Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL) aboard the International Space Station (ISS).
The approach for the investigation comprises two main aspects: the creation of ultracold atomic gases and the subsequent manipulation and probing of these gases to study systematic mitigations.

Creation of Ultracold Atomic Gas:
    Laser Cooling: Atoms are released into a vacuum chamber and collected into a magneto-optical trap (MOT), where they are initially cooled using laser cooling techniques. This technique cools the sample by essentially ‘pushing’ the laser against the motion of the atoms effectively lowering the momentum, and therefore the velocity, kinetic energy, and temperature, of the sample. This is followed by a brief stage of “optical molasses” during which the quadrupole magnetic field is turned off and the laser frequency is further detuned.
    State Preparation: The sample is optically pumped to a low magnetic field-seeking quantum state. The state is then subjected to an intermediate quadrupole magnetic field which allows for the transferred from the MOT, located at the bottom of the science region, to an atom-chip-based trap, located at the top of the science region.
    Evaporative Cooling: The atoms undergo evaporative cooling to below the critical temperature, leading to the formation of a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC). Evaporative cooling cools the sample by removing the highest-energy atom from the trap so that the remaining atoms re-thermalize at progressively lower temperatures.
    Decompression and Release: The atom trap is slightly relaxed to further cool the atoms, eventually releasing them completely to allow free expansion. At this point, investigators can search for the primary signatures of a BECs for confirmation that one was formed. Formation of a BEC is typically associated with the behavior of the velocity distribution, such as the presence of a narrow peak centered at near zero velocity and the low velocity peak growing (greater fraction of atoms in this domain) as the temperature decreases.

Manipulation and Probing:
    Probing Techniques: The ultracold gas is probed using laser, radiofrequency, and/or microwave pulses. These techniques are designed to investigate the pairwise interactions and molecular dynamics crucial for mitigating systematics in high-precision measurements.
    Time-of-Flight Imaging: After a predetermined time of flight, the sample is imaged using absorption imaging techniques. This process involves taking two images: one of the sample and a reference image post-destruction of the sample. As implied, this is the end of the experimental cycle for this specific BEC as the measurement process destroys the sample.

The manipulation and probing aspect is particularly important for this specific investigation as the ultracold atomic gas must be treated with certain fields to achieve the bubble topology. To do this, a locally harmonic spin-dependent trapping potential originating from inside an applied magnetic field is combined with a near-resonance oscillatory magnetic field with a certain radiofrequency. Together these two magnetic fields produce atoms in spatially dependent dressed atomic states that experience dressed or adiabatic dressed potentials. These potentials can then be manipulated via tuning the radiofrequency field so that the atoms take on an ellipsoid or bubble-like geometry.

## Research impacts

This work, while focused on the fundamental physics of ultracold atoms and not directly connected to human life, has a similar impact to life on Earth as that of all fundamental physics; it broadens our understanding of the physical world and helps us further cement our collective picture of quantum mechanics as ''the way the world works.'' It explores the limits of how large Bose-Einstein condensates can be made, and to what extent the gravity-well of terrestrial labs render certain investigations difficult or impossible. The observations made aboard CAL through this project are a clear demonstration that physical insight can sometimes require microgravity facilities to be fully developed, and that space borne atomic physics experiments can be valuable contributions to our collective scientific efforts. Additionally, insight into fundamental laws of quantum mechanics can have many applications in many on Earth. Quantum computers continue to exponentially accelerate as a field of study and in industry, and research into these fundamentals can support scientific workforce development, public science outreach and research and development.
