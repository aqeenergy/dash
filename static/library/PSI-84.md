# PSI-84 · MICS — Microgravity Investigation of Cement Solidification

- **Research area:** Materials Science
- **Sub-area:** Cement Solidification
- **Hardware:** Commercially available plastic bags (Burst Pouches®) with two separate compartments divided by an internal and burstable seal allowing material mixing under sealed conditions.
- **Flight platform:** International Space Station (ISS)
- **Project type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Keywords:** Microgravity · Diffusion controlled crystal growth · Porosity · MICS · Hydration · Cement Solidification · Density · Cement

## Objective

The objective of the proposed investigation is to better understand the complex process of cement solidification by conducting experiments in the microgravity environment aboard the International Space Station (ISS) and further the development of an in-situ lunar concrete material.

## Hypothesis

The research hypothesis for the ISS experiments is that minimizing gravity-driven transport phenomena, such as buoyancy, sedimentation, and convection results in a purely diffusion-controlled hydration process. Without the physical effects induced by gravity, diffusion will lead to a more uniform distribution of phases. As such, the dissolving cement particles will disperse in the aqueous solution, resulting in greater uniformity of features such as pore structures and crystal morphology in the hardened cement matrix. A different microstructure is expected to form in microgravity (µg), which possibly leads to different physical properties of µg-hardened cement paste with respect to that hardened in terrestrial gravity (1g).

## Approach

Several different mixture combinations of tricalcium aluminate (C3A), tricalcium silicate (C3S), ordinary portland cement (OPC), superplasticizer (SP) and ratios of cement to water were packed in commercially available plastic bags (Burst Pouches®) with separate compartments divided by an internal, burstable seal which allowed mixing of materials under sealed conditions in microgravity and terrestrial conditions. Space and ground samples were mixed simultaneously and at the same conditions of 20 ºC ± 2 ºC, 1 ATM and 35% RH. As such, gravity is considered the only variable between the space and ground experiments. After a specific period of hydration samples were subjected to a wide variety of characterization techniques such as image analysis and mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) to assess porosity and pore size distribution, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) to estimate degree of hydration, x-ray diffraction (XRD) for crystalline phase identification, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and electron microprobe analysis (EMPA) for compositional analysis. Phase distribution, pore structures, and crystal morphology as a function of composition and gravity level was documented.

## Research impacts

The MICS investigation directly addresses the high priority need for lightweight space structures for habitats that protect crew. Results from this investigation enable the characterization of microstructural development in the hardening of cement paste and improve understanding of crystal growth and hydration kinetics, phase formations, pore distribution, and material properties. Also, MICS can increase understanding and control of the crystal growth kinetics, and morphology, to improve concrete properties. Improved cement properties can lead to improved concrete properties, durability, and lower CO2 emissions.
