# PSI-182 — Colloidal gels:  Aging in microgravity, Structure of highly polydisperse gels

- **Research area:** Complex Fluids - Soft Matter
- **Sub-area:** Colloids
- **Project type:** Ground Investigation
- **Related investigations:** PSI-13

## Objective

Data are from two projects:  one related to aging, one related to polydispersity.  The first project has data under folders with a name containing AGING.  The second has data under folders with a name containing POLYDISPERSE.

-------

Project 1: Objectives specific to aging of colloid gels
•Quantify coarsening of colloidal gels under microgravity conditions.
•Quantify changes in thermal fluctuations of colloidal gels under microgravity conditions.
•Understand aging of colloidal gels over time scales range from seconds to tens of hours.

Project 2: Objectives related to structure of polydisperse colloidal gels
•Measure fractal dimension of polydisperse colloidal gels (on Earth)
•Understand how particle size relates to contact number
•Quantify tetrahedral structures, and how particle size influences tetrahedral structure in colloidal gels

## Hypothesis

Project 1:  Colloidal gels in microgravity conditions will coarsen over time, and their thermal fluctuations will diminish over time as the gel gets coarser; the coarsening will continue indefinitely because gravitational collapse will not be possible.

Project 2:  Large particles play significant roles in highly polydisperse colloidal gels, as they serve as highly connected nodes in the structure.

## Approach

Project 1: We studied colloidal gels using the data from the Advanced Colloids Experiment-Microscopy-1 (ACE-M1) experiments. Colloidal gels are formed from sticky micron-sized solid particles in a liquid, where over time the particles stick together and form random clumps that eventually bridge across the entire sample chamber in tangled networks. The ACE-M1 data set consists of a large number of optical microscopy movies of colloidal gels. In microgravity, these gels are long-lived, rather than collapsing under their own weight as happens in ground-based experiments. We have analyzed these microscopy movies using particle tracking, studying the visible thermal fluctuations and the gel structure as it evolves over time. Some gels have thick aggregated regions, while others have more tenuous gel strands and free particles. We show that the structure and dynamics change over the several-day period of each experiment.

Project 2: In a second project, we did ground-based experiments to study the structure of colloidal gels composed of polydisperse particles.  In particular, we used confocal microscopy to image colloidal gels formed from highly polydisperse particles. We suspended our polydisperse particles in a density matched solvent, and let the particles spontaneously aggregate through the van der Waals force. The largest particles were more than 15 times the size of the smallest particles.  As expected, larger particles have more nearest neighbors than small ones.  This leads to an over-representation of large particles in tetrahedral structures where four particles are mutually nearest neighbors, showing that large particles help provide rigidity to the gel structure.  The tetrahedral structures also suggest that particles are able to rearrange somewhat during the gelation process, until their motion is stabilized by the multiple contacts with their neighbors.

## Research impacts

This research helps us understand the technical challenge of keeping particles dispersed in a colloidal suspension.  A gel is one way to prevent particles from settling, although the long-time behavior of a gel is challenging to study under Earth gravity.
