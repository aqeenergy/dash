# PSI-138 — Pool Boiling Heat Transfer Mechanisms in Low Gravity: Numerical Experiments of MABE and NPBX Data

- **Research area:** Fluid Physics
- **Sub-area:** Boiling and Condensation
- **Project type:** Ground Investigation
- **Related investigations:** PSI-85 PSI-81
- **Keywords:** MABE · Pool boiling · Heat flux coupling · Nucleate boiling mechanisms · NPBX · Buble-dynamics · Microlayer evaporation behavior

## Objective

The primary objectives for this PSI grant include establishing a deeper mechanistic understanding of nucleate boiling under low-gravity conditions by conducting high-fidelity numerical simulations that replicate the flight conditions of prior ISS-based boiling experiments, specifically MABE and NPBX. The investigation seeks to characterize key mechanisms governing bubble growth, waiting time, vapor spreading, coalescence, and detachment under variable gravity levels, degrees of wall superheat, and levels of liquid subcooling. Central to the objectives is quantifying how heat flux divides between nucleate boiling and natural convection modes, evaluating how the contact angle modulates wall adhesion forces, and determining the precise contribution of microlayer evaporation to heat transfer during low-gravity operation. By leveraging validated direct-numerical-simulation approaches tied to empirical MABE and NPBX measurements, this objective generates fundamental physics-based insights that enable predictive modeling of boiling processes relevant to spacecraft systems and advanced thermal architectures.

## Hypothesis

The research was based on the hypothesis that, in microgravity, shifts in the relative influence of buoyancy, capillary forces, and microlayer evaporation fundamentally alter bubble-growth behavior and reduce heat-flux performance. It further hypothesized that these low-gravity effects can be accurately captured using high-fidelity Direct Numerical Simulations (DNS) validated against historic ISS boiling data from the MABE and NPBX experiments. By exploring variations in gravity, surface wettability, and thermal conditions, the study aimed to test the mechanisms governing bubble dynamics and heat transfer for application to spacecraft thermal management.

## Approach

The project approach was to use validated high-fidelity Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) ground-based computational simulations to numerically reproduce MABE- and NPBX-like boiling environments over a controlled range of heater temperatures, wall superheat conditions, and fluid subcooling levels. The research draws directly from ISS-acquired datasets that include detailed bubble growth dynamics, heat-flux profiles, and heater-surface imaging, which serve as reference benchmarks that guide simulation fidelity. Multiphase DNS simulations incorporate fluid phase-change physics, contact-line motion, interphase mass transport, and three-dimensional flow-field resolution to connect bubble behavior to instantaneous and time-averaged heat transfer. Iterative simulation campaigns then probe how variable gravitational acceleration, wettability contrast, bubble confinement, and microlayer evaporation influence overall heat-flux partitioning. The resulting database enables first-principles analysis of changes in boiling performance between terrestrial and microgravity settings while supporting quantitative model development applicable to spaceflight hardware, thermal architectures, and advanced electronic cooling systems.

## Research impacts

Space Benefits: This investigation provides high-resolution insight into how boiling heat-transfer efficiency degrades under reduced gravity, enabling the development of more reliable spacecraft thermal-management systems that do not depend solely on buoyancy-driven vapor removal. Improved understanding of microlayer-based transport, contact-line wetting, spatial heat-flux distribution, and boiling-mode transition directly supports the design of future ISS and deep-space thermal architectures, power-electronics packaging, life-support thermal management, and compact, energy-efficient cooling platforms for extended-duration missions.


Earth Benefits: The computational foundations and physics-based insights gained through this study directly advance terrestrial thermal-management strategies for high-power electronic systems, including server-class processors, power-dense computing architectures, and high-performance energy-conversion systems. The work improves predictive control of boiling-induced heat removal, enabling higher operating throughput, greater component longevity, and reduced power consumption in heat-intensive commercial and defense electronics.
