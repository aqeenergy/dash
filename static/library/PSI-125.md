## Objective

The primary objective of this investigation was to understand why protein crystallization experiments in microgravity have generated unexpectedly low or high numbers of crystals. Each outcome may cause experiments designed to obtain a small number of well-separated crystals to fail.

The objective of the proposed investigations is to explore the effects of a hitherto unstudied factor for the nucleation of protein crystals: solution shear flow. Besides providing a novel tool for nucleation control for protein crystallization experiments, the correlation between solution flows and protein crystal nucleation, to be tested in the proposed investigation, will shed light on one of the major mysteries of current space biophysics: the finding that in many cases the number of crystals nucleated in microgravity was greater or smaller than those nucleated on earth.

## Hypothesis

Hypothesis: The behavior of dense liquid clusters at different rates of convection will have a profound effect on crystal growth.

The main hypothesis underlying the proposed investigations consists of two parts:
1. The existence of nucleation precursors and the correlation between their properties and the nucleation rate can be used to control protein crystal nucleation via solution flow.
2. Enhancement or suppression of nucleation via solution flow, i.e., independently of solution supersaturation, can be used to achieve higher perfection of the grown crystals.
3. A very small fraction of the protein exists in a dense liquid phase in the form of Brownian particles, each a cluster of roughly 105 protein molecules undergoing liquid-like dynamics.
4. Crystal nucleation occurs when a small, ordered domain forms within the highly concentrated liquid-state environment of one of these particles.

## Approach

Three cassettes containing five capillaries each were loaded with one of three proteins; (1) Lysozyme, (2) p53, and (3) hnRNPA-LC. The observation of these protein clusters were observed with a newly developed technique, called differential dynamic microscopy (DDM). DDM improves a recent method used to study and quantify the dynamics of sub-micrometer particles in solutions from fluctuations in intensity in optical micrographs. All samples were frozen to a temperature of -80C before flight to the ISS. The proteins were thawed and observed over several weeks in the ISS LMM module where they were filmed and photographed. Sample details and experimental parameters are summarized in LMMBio2_Experimental Table in the science document section.

## Research impacts

These images provide data that help scientists and engineers understand the forces that control organization and dynamics of matter at microscopic scales. The organization and movement of matter on the microscopic level profoundly affects the macroscopic world and understanding such processes contributes to development of more efficient materials and machines for application on Earth.
