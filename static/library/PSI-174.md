# PSI-174 — Micromechanics Based Modeling of Cement Solidified in the Microgravity

- **Research area:** Materials Science
- **Sub-area:** Cement hydration
- **Project type:** Ground Investigation
- **Keywords:** grant · scanning electron microscopy · micromechanics analysis code with generalized method of cells · micromechanics-based modeling · MICS · cement

## Objective

Micromechanics‑Based Modeling of Cement Solidified in the Microgravity aimed to advance knowledge of the mechanical properties of cement binders formed in reduced gravity by leveraging microgravity materials data, such as SEM/BSE micrographs, available in the PSI database.
This project sought to connect microstructure (e.g., porosity, phase morphology such as portlandite) observed in ISS‑cured tricalcium silicate (C3S) pastes to bulk mechanical/transport behavior through modeling, thereby informing design and engineering of cementitious materials for extraterrestrial construction and providing validated simulation data that scale sparse flight samples to application‑relevant sizes.

## Hypothesis

Consistent with the microgravity MICS investigation, the central hypothesis was that minimizing gravity‑driven transport (buoyancy, sedimentation, convection) yields a diffusion‑controlled hydration process in microgravity, producing more uniform phase distributions and distinct microstructures (e.g., higher porosity, altered portlandite morphology) relative to 1g; these differences are expected to translate into measurably different mechanical properties for µg‑hardened cement paste versus terrestrial cement. The grant tested this by quantifying microstructural features from flight/ground samples and propagating them through micromechanics to predict property deltas attributable to gravity effects.

## Approach

In this work, the micromechanics analysis code with generalized method of cells (MAC/GMC) was used to predict the structure-property relationship of these unique cement samples.
First, the sparse data of cement hydrated in microgravity environment was converted to high-fidelity 3D microstructure with the aid of a deep learning-based microstructure methodology. The reconstructed sub-volumes were compared against micro-CT data using statistical descriptors. Second, the sub-volumes (partitioned based on individual constituents) of such reconstructed 3D structures were fed into the NASA Multiscale Analysis Tool (NASMAT) to obtain homogenized properties of the cement. A semi-automated Python script was developed to complete the workflow.  Owing to the high computational demand, the analyses were carried out in NASA Advanced Supercomputing (NAS) servers.

Phase‑discerned ISS and ground BSE/SEM images were segmented and reconstructed into 3D volumes using a deep learning microstructure framework; these Representative Volume Elements (RVEs) were then analyzed with NASA’s Multiscale Analysis Tool (NASMAT) to homogenize and predict effective properties. Reconstruction quality was validated against statistical descriptors and micro‑CT data, and the modeling spans length‑scale partitioning to capture porous, multiphase behavior.
The experimental basis originated from the MICS investigation (PSI‑84 in the database), which conducted controlled ISS/ground hydration in sealed Burst Pouches, keeping temperature/pressure/humidity constant so gravity is the sole variable, followed by porosimetry, TGA, XRD, EDS/EMPA, and image analysis to quantify phase fractions and pore structures that feed the modeling pipeline.

## Research impacts

This research provides the first reference simulation data and a practical route to predict how cement (including regolith‑based mixes) behaves when fabricated in space, which can inform Artemis‑aligned or other space-based habitat concepts and construction logistics where transporting bulk materials is prohibitive.
On Earth, the deep-learning-assisted reconstruction and micromechanics workflow developed can be applied to other multiphase materials, enabling materials-by-design even when only a limited samples are available. By clarifying how processing conditions in microgravity affect cement structure and performance, such as the ~70% porosity and plate-like portlandite observed in ISS samples, this work improves predictive models for optimizing mix designs and curing conditions in constrained environments. These advances also benefit low-resource or extreme-environment construction on Earth and contribute more broadly to the ICME community.
