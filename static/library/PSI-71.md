## Objective

The primary objective of the microgravity dendritic growth experiment was to measure definitively the kinetics and morphology of dendritic growth, and to account for the effects of natural convection. The experimental objective was to use a single,clearly defined experimental parameter, namely, the supercooling, to fully determine the resulting steady-state dendritic growth velocity, V, and tip radius R. Designated the IDGE, the first flight, manifested on the second United States Microgravity Payload (USMP-2) mission, was launched and operated on the STS-62 on March 4-18, 1994. This IDGE investigation met the objective of acquiring accurate dendritic growth speed and morphology data under diffusion-controlled conditions, while greatly reducing buoyancy-induced convective heat transport through the reduction of gravity. The microgravity environment broadened this range of supercooling and permitted for the first time a critical assessment of both the heat transport theory and the interfacial kinetics behavior.

## Hypothesis

The hypothesis of this investigation is that as a function of the supercooling there exists an explicit dendritic dynamic operating state defined by dendritic tip velocity, V and the dendrite tip paraboloidal radius of curvature, R. The Peclet number obtained from the Ivantsov solution for each specified supercooling yielded an infinite number of solutions for V and R. Therefore it is hypothesized that a second independent equation or length scale exists, which, when combined with the Ivantsov diffusion solution, will mathematically select the observed unique dynamic operating state. Two of the candidates for the additional crystal growth physics that could provide the second  crucial equation are the effects from the solid-melt interfacial energy, the influence of temperature fluctuations in  the melt, or both.

## Approach

The approach was to observe and image dendritic growth as a function of undercooling such that dendritic tip velocities and radii could both be accurately measured and used as a critical test of theory. Succinonitrile (SCN), transparent when molten, was used as a model metal analog system for studying dendritic growth since it solidifies like the body centered cubic (BCC) ferrous metal alloys, i.e., with an atomically ''rough'' solid-liquid interface. In addition, SCN displays convenient properties for solidification experiments, such as a low melting temperature (58.08 °C), optical transparency, and the potential for accurate characterization of its thermophysical properties. Dendritic growth is initiated inside the experimental chamber by energizing thermoelectric coolers attached to one end a thin capillary injector tube called the stinger. The other end of the stinger, where the dendrite emerges in the center of the chamber, is notched, much like the tip of a fountain pen. This helps isolate the leading dendrite and reduces the time needed until the leading dendrite is isothermal and has achieved steady state.
The IDGE flight experiment was divided into 60 operational cycles. The first cycle, the warm-up cycle, consists of bringing the system up to the operational temperature where all system components are checked and initialized. The second cycle measured the liquidus temperature of the SCN as a calibration point from which all subsequent supercoolings are measured. The remaining cycles were dendritic growth cycles where dendrites are grown and photographed at varying supercoolings. For each dendritic growth cycle, the experiment obtained a time series of stereo-pairs of 35-mm photographic images, low-resolution, binary, digitized electronic images and various data streams characterizing the dendrite's growth conditions during the cycle. For each image pair, either 35-mm film negative or digitized image, we measured the position of the dendrite tip, and the associated time that the image was recorded. The dendritic velocity was determined by calculating the slope of the displacement-time curve from the steady-state regime of the growth cycle. The velocity is then adjusted by appropriate stereographic and magnification factors. This procedure yields a set of dendrite velocity measurements accurate to about ± 2 percent.

## Research impacts

The size, shape, spacing, and orientation of the dendrites in cast metal alloys have a major effect on the strength, ductility and toughness. Nearly all cast-metal alloys used in everyday products, such as automobiles and airplanes, are composed of dendritic microstructures. Metallurgists have long sought to predict and control alloy microstructures. The development of automated, cost effective manufacturing techniques ultimately depends on the precision with which the problem in non-equilibrium pattern formation can be solved. In principle, the intent is to incorporate fundamental understanding of microstructures into computer codes that simultaneously help  design materials with made to order properties and optimize their manufacturability and performance. Dendritic growth experiments conducted in the microgravity environment can provide benchmark data to validate such predictive models.
