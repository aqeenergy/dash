# PSI-179 · ASE — Aerosol Sampling Experiment

- **Research area:** Complex Fluids - Soft Matter
- **Sub-area:** Multiphase Flow
- **Hardware:** Passive Aerosol Samplers (PAS)
- **Flight platform:** International Space Station (ISS)
- **Project type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Keywords:** aerosols · Thermophysical properties

## Objective

Aerosols are small particles suspended in the air, and in Earth’s atmosphere, aerosols include soot, dust, pollen and a wide range of other natural and human-made materials. Smoke does not rise and dust does not settle in microgravity the way they do on Earth, causing aerosols to behave differently and pose hazards for crew members breathing the air. The Aerosol Sampling Experiment (ASE) collects airborne particles in the International Space Station’s (ISS) cabin air, and returns them to Earth for scientists to study.

The objectives of ASE were:
1- Characterize airborne particulate matter aboard the ISS to assess air quality for crew health and hardware longevity.
2- Identify particle sources (e.g., human skin, equipment wear, biological contaminants) in the spacecraft environment.
3- Improve understanding of particle transport and accumulation in microgravity conditions.
4- Support design of future spacecraft air filtration systems, ensuring they are appropriately targeted for actual airborne particle profiles.

## Hypothesis

Airborne particulate matter aboard the ISS exhibits a different size distribution, composition, and transport behavior in microgravity compared to Earth-based environments, and that particles from crew activity and hardware wear contribute significantly to the aerosol burden as well as the assumption that microgravity reduces sedimentation, allowing finer particles to stay suspended longer and accumulate in regions not typically affected by gravity-based settling on Earth.

## Approach

The ASE approach involved passive aerosol sampling in multiple ISS modules using filters placed in air ducts and cabin areas (especially near high-traffic zones and vents) by deploying commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) samplers for long-duration exposure (3 months–1 year).  The sampled filters were returned to Earth for ground-based analysis using: 1- Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), 2- Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) and 3- Gravimetric analysis. The ISS samples were compared to terrestrial cleanroom and indoor environments to assess relative cleanliness and particle source uniqueness.  Data collected was cross-referenced with previous ISS air monitoring efforts (e.g., TOCA, CCAA) to inform models of contaminant accumulation and mitigation.

## Research impacts

Crew members on the ISS are breathing more or less the same fixed volume of air that has been on the ISS for years. There is no chance to get ‘fresh air’ as we do on Earth, but an advanced air revitalization system processes and cleans the air continuously. Crew members in space have complained of allergies and nose and eye irritation, which suggests the ISS cabin air has a high concentration of tiny particles that can be inhaled. The ISS filtration system is designed to remove these particles, but researchers have no way to verify the efficiency or cleanliness of the air. This investigation generates new data to characterize the quality of the air on the ISS, in terms of particles. Future space travelers may benefit from this data, as they can be used in the design and selection of particle detectors appropriate for low gravity. Long-term manned space missions need aerosol measurement capability to ensure crew health and comfort
